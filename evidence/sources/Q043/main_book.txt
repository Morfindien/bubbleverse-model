Preface
Cosmology is often presented as a sequence of established facts: the Universe expands,
distant galaxies recede, the cosmic microwave background records an earlier thermal
state, and a small set of parameters describes the large-scale evolution of the cosmos. That
presentation is useful, but it can hide a more fundamental scientific question: how much of
this picture follows directly from observation, how much depends on inference, and where
do the assumptions enter?
This book approaches cosmology by rebuilding the argument from the evidence outward.
The aim is not to begin with a preferred model and search for observations that support it.
Instead, the investigation asks what can actually be established, which conclusions depend
on a particular theoretical framework, which measurements are genuinely independent,
and which uncertainties remain capable of changing the interpretation.
This distinction matters because cosmological observations rarely measure the quantities
of greatest theoretical interest directly. A redshift is observed. An angle is observed. A flux
is observed. A spectrum is observed. A telescope records photons. A spectrograph records
wavelengths. An interferometer records changes in optical path length. From these signals,
distances, expansion rates, densities, ages, masses, and model parameters are
reconstructed through layers of calibration, physical modelling, and statistical inference.
Beyond those reconstructions lie still deeper interpretations: dark matter, dark energy, the
hot early Universe, inflation, and the cosmological framework used to connect them.
Precision does not make every quantity equally direct.
A scientifically useful reconstruction of the Universe must therefore preserve the boundary
between what an instrument measures and what a model allows us to conclude from that
measurement. A successful model may provide an extraordinarily accurate description of
observations without automatically becoming the final physical explanation. Conversely,
the absence of a directly identified microscopic mechanism does not erase the observations
that motivated the model.
The investigation begins with the empirical ground beneath those questions. It first asks
what is actually observed, then moves outward toward reconstructed expansion,
disagreements among inference chains, proposed explanations, and the computational
tests used to examine them.
The working rule throughout the book is simple:
Start with the signal. Preserve the measurement. Make every inferential step visible.

Chapter 1 — What We Actually Observe
The Observable Universe
Astronomy does not provide a view of the universe from outside. Every empirical
statement begins inside one causally accessible region: the observable universe.
Light and other signals require time to travel. There is therefore a limit to the region from
which information can have reached us during cosmic history. Observations can constrain
the contents, geometry, dynamics, and past evolution of this observable region, but they do
not directly determine the size or topology of the universe as a whole.
This distinction rules out several claims that observation alone cannot establish. We do not
directly know whether the entire universe is finite or infinite. We do not observe a physical
outer edge. We do not observe a spatial centre from which cosmic expansion proceeds. Nor
can observations inside our causal region determine everything that may exist beyond it.
The universe and the observable universe must therefore not be treated as synonyms.
Within the region we can observe, however, the evidence is extraordinarily rich.
Matter is distributed hierarchically across an enormous range of scales. We observe
planets, stars, stellar remnants, gas, dust, galaxies, galaxy groups, galaxy clusters,
filaments, and vast underdense regions. The distribution is strongly inhomogeneous on
small and intermediate scales, while sufficiently large volumes approach statistical
homogeneity and isotropy.
This large-scale structure is not a theoretical abstraction. Modern spectroscopic surveys
measure the positions and redshifts of millions of galaxies and quasars, allowing the three-
dimensional distribution of cosmic structure to be reconstructed statistically. The second
major data release of the Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument, for example, uses more
than fourteen million galaxies and quasars in its baryon acoustic oscillation analysis.
The universe is therefore not observed as a smooth static background. It is observed as
structured, evolving, and dynamic.
Looking Backward in Time
Because light travels at a finite speed, distance also provides access to earlier cosmic
epochs.
A nearby galaxy is seen relatively recently in its history. A very distant galaxy is observed
as it existed billions of years ago. Deep observations therefore function as a form of cosmic
archaeology: different distances expose different stages of the universe’s development.
This does not mean that astronomers literally watch a single galaxy evolve over billions of
years. Instead, populations observed at different redshifts reveal systematic changes in
properties such as star-formation activity, galaxy populations, chemical enrichment,
quasar activity, clustering, and the abundance of massive structures.

The observation of systematic evolution with redshift is itself empirical. Translating a
particular redshift into an exact cosmic age requires a cosmological model.
That distinction will recur frequently: the signal can be direct even when the conversion
into a physical historical quantity is model dependent.
The Cosmic Microwave Background
One of the most important signals in all of cosmology is present in every direction in the
sky.
The cosmic microwave background, or CMB, is an almost isotropic field of microwave
radiation whose spectrum is extraordinarily close to that of a thermal blackbody. A
precision determination of its mean thermodynamic temperature gives
T C M B=2.72548±0.00057 K .
The temperature is not perfectly uniform. Tiny direction-dependent fluctuations are
observed, together with a characteristic pattern of polarization.
These fluctuations are usually analysed statistically through angular power spectra.
Modern CMB experiments measure temperature correlations and polarization
combinations conventionally denoted TT, TE, and EE. Their spectra contain a sequence of
prominent acoustic peaks.
These peaks are observations in the statistical structure of the microwave sky.
Their physical interpretation is a separate step.
Within the standard hot-universe picture, the pattern is understood as the imprint of
acoustic oscillations in a tightly coupled photon–baryon plasma before photons were able
to propagate freely through the universe. This interpretation is exceptionally successful,
but the distinction remains important: detectors observe microwave photons, anisotropies,
polarization, and angular correlations. The early plasma is the physical explanation
constructed from those observations.
Planck measured this structure with high precision, while the Atacama Cosmology
Telescope provides an independent high-resolution measurement of the CMB temperature
and polarization spectra.
The CMB therefore provides both a direct observed radiation field and one of the most
powerful model-dependent windows onto the physical conditions of the early universe.
It is not, however, a direct observation of a singularity at t=0.

The scientific question is consequently more precise than asking whether distant galaxies
are moving away from us. We must ask whether observations made at different distances
and cosmic epochs are consistent with a common evolving scale factor.
It is useful to keep several quantities separate: observed wavelength, redshift, distance,
recession velocity, the cosmic scale factor, H (z ), and the present expansion parameter H 0.
They are related, but they are not identical measurements.
The crucial distinction is between observation and inference. Redshifts, fluxes, angular
separations, spectral features, and time intervals are observational quantities. A
reconstructed function such as H (z ), by contrast, is generally inferred from those
observations under specified assumptions.
The evidence for expansion is therefore stronger than the existence of cosmological
redshifts alone. The relevant question is whether physically different observations of
redshift, distance, and time can be described consistently by the same evolving cosmic
geometry.

This separation between measurement and geometry is not semantic nitpicking. Later
questions about the Hubble constant and dark energy depend critically on knowing where
the measurements end and the cosmological assumptions begin.
Supernovae and the Discovery of Late-Time Acceleration
Type Ia supernovae provide one of the clearest examples of the distinction between an
observed relation and the physical conclusion drawn from it.
Their light curves can be standardized sufficiently well to compare their relative
luminosity distances across cosmological redshift.
The original high-redshift supernova programmes led by Riess and collaborators and by
Perlmutter and collaborators independently found that distant Type Ia supernovae were
inconsistent with the distance–redshift relation expected in simple matter-dominated, non-
accelerating cosmologies.
Modern samples are vastly larger. Pantheon+ contains 1,701 light curves corresponding to
1,550 distinct Type Ia supernovae over approximately
0.001<z<2.26.
The primary observation is the standardized supernova distance–redshift relation.
Within Friedmann–Lemaître–Robertson–Walker cosmology and general relativity, that
relation strongly supports a period of accelerated late-time cosmic expansion.
The acceleration is therefore a powerful physical inference, but it does not by itself identify
its cause.
The term dark energy is used for the component, field, modification, or effective
phenomenon responsible for reproducing this accelerated behaviour in a cosmological
model. The name does not imply that a dark-energy particle or field has been directly
isolated experimentally.
The observations establish the expansion history far more securely than they establish the
microscopic explanation for it.
Supernovae constrain the integrated expansion history rather than measuring H (z ) at a
single redshift. This distinction is important. A luminosity-distance relation can provide
powerful evidence for an evolving cosmic geometry, but recovering a local expansion rate
from it requires additional inference. The observed distance–redshift relation and the
reconstructed expansion history should therefore not be treated as identical quantities.
Baryon Acoustic Oscillations
The large-scale distribution of matter contains another striking statistical feature: a
preferred clustering scale known as the baryon acoustic oscillation, or BAO, scale.
The feature is measured in the spatial correlations of galaxies and quasars and can also be
extracted from the Lyman-α forest.
BUBBLEVERSE MAIN BOOK
0.42
DESI has measured this structure across several redshift ranges with enormous
spectroscopic samples.
The clustering feature itself is observed.
Its use as a cosmic standard ruler introduces additional physics.
In the standard interpretation, the scale is the late-time remnant of acoustic propagation in
the photon–baryon plasma of the early universe. The physical size of that ruler is
commonly related to the sound horizon rd.
The radial BAO measurement is especially valuable for reconstructing expansion because
the observed redshift separation of the acoustic feature is related to the combination
H (z )rd. BAO therefore does not determine H (z ) in complete isolation. Converting the
observed ruler into an absolute expansion rate requires either an external calibration of rd
or assumptions about the early-universe physics that determines it.
This dependence does not remove BAO as evidence for an evolving distance–redshift
geometry. Instead, it identifies precisely where early-universe physics enters the absolute
calibration. That distinction will become especially important when different
determinations of the cosmic expansion rate are compared.
Gravitational Lensing
Gravity does not merely affect the motion of matter. It also changes the propagation of
light.
Foreground mass can distort, magnify, multiply image, or statistically shear radiation
emitted by more distant objects. This phenomenon, gravitational lensing, is observed
across a wide range of scales.
Lensing can therefore be used to reconstruct information about the integrated gravitating
mass along the line of sight.
The CMB itself is gravitationally lensed by intervening cosmic structure. Planck
reconstructed this lensing field with high statistical significance, providing a direct
statistical probe of the matter distribution between the surface of last scattering and the
observer.
The signal is particularly valuable because it depends on gravitating matter rather than
merely on emitted light.
The Missing-Mass Phenomenon
One of the most persistent empirical facts in modern astrophysics is that the gravitational
behaviour of many systems cannot be accounted for by the observed luminous baryonic
material alone within conventional gravitational modelling.
Galaxy rotation curves provide one major example.
BUBBLEVERSE MAIN BOOK
0.42
In the SPARC galaxy sample, measurements of rotationally supported galaxies reveal a
tight relation between the total centripetal acceleration inferred from the motion of matter
and the acceleration expected from the observed baryonic distribution. At low
accelerations the two cease to agree in the way a baryon-only Newtonian model would
predict.
Galaxy clusters provide a physically different test.
In systems such as the Bullet Cluster, the dominant X-ray-emitting baryonic plasma can be
spatially displaced from the dominant gravitating mass reconstructed from lensing. This
creates a separation between where most of the ordinary gaseous matter is observed and
where the gravitational mass is inferred to lie.
Together with CMB and large-scale-structure constraints, these observations motivate the
standard conclusion that the universe contains a substantial non-luminous gravitating
component.
The safest empirical statement is nevertheless more specific than the everyday phrase
“dark matter has been observed.”
What has been observed are gravitational effects that require either additional gravitating
mass or a successful alternative description of gravity capable of reproducing the same
body of phenomena.
A predominantly non-baryonic dark-matter component is currently the most successful
common explanation across the full cosmological framework.
Its microscopic identity remains unknown.
A WIMP, axion, ultralight field, sterile neutrino, or other proposed dark-matter candidate
should therefore not be promoted to observational fact until independent evidence
identifies it.
The gravitational phenomenon is robust. The particle identity is not.
Gravitational Waves
Cosmology and astrophysics no longer rely exclusively on electromagnetic radiation.
In 2015 the LIGO detectors recorded GW150914, a transient strain signal detected by two
interferometers and consistent with the merger of two compact objects described as
stellar-mass black holes within general relativity.
This was the first direct detection of gravitational waves.
GW170817 later provided a gravitational-wave signal from a binary neutron-star inspiral
together with electromagnetic counterparts. Its network signal-to-noise ratio was
approximately 32.4.
These events opened a genuinely different observational channel.
BUBBLEVERSE MAIN BOOK
0.42
The detectors do not observe an image of spacetime. They record correlated changes in
interferometer arm lengths whose temporal structure is consistent with propagating
gravitational waves.
The interpretation of the sources relies on relativistic waveform modelling, but the
existence of gravitational-wave signals is now an observational fact.
Modern astronomy is consequently a multi-messenger discipline. Information reaches us
through electromagnetic radiation, gravitational waves, neutrinos, and high-energy
particles.
Independent channels matter because conclusions supported by different physics are
harder to dismiss as artefacts of a single observational method.
Chemical Evidence from the Early Universe
Spectroscopy also reveals the chemical composition of astronomical matter.
Hydrogen, helium, deuterium, and heavier elements leave identifiable absorption and
emission features. Particularly metal-poor absorption systems can be used to estimate
primordial light-element abundances.

Its interpretation as a primordial abundance and its connection to Big Bang

Within standard nuclear and cosmological physics, light-element abundances provide a
strikingly successful test of a universe that once existed in a much hotter and denser state.
The inference reaches much further back in cosmic history than direct electromagnetic
imaging, but it is correspondingly less direct.
This distinction is useful: observation can preserve information about an epoch without
giving us a photograph of that epoch.
A Hotter and Denser Past
Several physically different observations converge on the conclusion that the universe was
once much hotter and denser than it is today.
The most important lines include cosmic expansion, the cosmic microwave background,
and primordial light-element abundances.
The power of this conclusion does not arise from any one observation in isolation.
Expansion implies that matter and radiation occupied a denser configuration in the past.
The thermal microwave background supplies an observed relic radiation field. Nuclear
abundances are consistent with reactions expected in a sufficiently hot early environment.

These pieces form an unusually coherent empirical picture.
This strongly supports the hot Big Bang description of the observable universe’s early
evolution.
It does not demonstrate a physical singularity.
A mathematical extrapolation toward a→0 within classical general relativity is not itself
an observation of an initial point of infinite density. The observational evidence establishes
a hot, dense early phase; what happened arbitrarily close to the beginning of the classical
extrapolation remains a deeper theoretical question.

Chapter 4 — Testing Early-Universe Solutions
What a Successful Solution Must Do
The Hubble tension cannot be solved merely by producing a model in which the inferred
value of H₀ moves upward.
A successful early-Universe explanation must alter the calibration linking early-time
physics to later cosmological distances while remaining compatible with the rest of the
observations. The same model must still account for the measured cosmic microwave
background, baryon acoustic oscillations, Big Bang nucleosynthesis constraints, and the
other data used in the inference.
This is why reducing the sound horizon is necessary for many early-Universe proposals but
is not sufficient. Changing one part of the cosmological ruler forces compensating changes
elsewhere.
The Bubbleverse calculations therefore tested not only whether a mechanism could reach a
higher H₀, but what happened to the complete likelihood while it did so.
n=3 Early Dark Energy
Early dark energy, or EDE, provides one way of changing the pre-recombination expansion
history. In the n=3 model examined here, a temporary additional energy component can
reduce the effective early-Universe distance scale and allow higher inferred values of H ₀.
A recent profile-likelihood analysis discussed in this investigation reports a region around
f_EDE = 0.09 ± 0.03 and H₀ = 71.0 ± 1.1 km s⁻¹ Mpc⁻¹. The Bubbleverse calculations likewise
found higher-H₀ regions, but the numerical history showed why the existence of such a
region is not enough to establish a solution.

profile and apparently large penalties at some points. Later multi-start and deterministic

The comparison between n=3 EDE and varying electron mass reveals one of the central

and likelihood components.
The scientific target is therefore not simply a preferred value of H ₀.

The comparison was pushed one level deeper without manufacturing a synthetic hybrid

Chapter 5 — What the Model Explains— and What Remains Unknown
Dark Energy Is an Explanation, Not a Detected Substance
The same epistemic care applied to dark matter and the Hubble constant also applies to
dark energy.
Supernovae and large-scale-structure distance measurements strongly support late-time
accelerated expansion within the relevant relativistic cosmological framework. ΛCDM
represents the cause of that acceleration through a cosmological constant.
Other models allow the effective dark-energy contribution to evolve.
DESI DR2 combinations with CMB and Type Ia supernova data provide model-dependent
evidence for time-varying dark-energy parameterizations over a pure cosmological
constant. The original DESI DR2 analysis reported preferences ranging from approximately
2.8σ to 4.2σ depending on the supernova compilation. Subsequent 2026 reanalyses using
updated host-galaxy information and recalibrated supernova samples bring the
comparisons into substantially closer agreement, at approximately 3.2 σ–3.4 σ in the w ₀w ₐ
plane.
This is scientifically interesting model-comparison evidence.
It is not a direct observation of a changing dark-energy field.
The result depends on the datasets being combined and on the parameterization used to
describe the possible evolution.
The physical origin of late-time acceleration therefore remains unresolved. A cosmological
constant remains viable, and dynamical dark energy remains an active possibility.
What ΛCDM Actually Achieves
ΛCDM deserves neither automatic belief nor casual dismissal.
Its empirical success is substantial.
With a small number of parameters, it provides a compact description of the cosmic
microwave background temperature and polarization spectra, the acoustic peak structure,
baryon acoustic oscillations, important aspects of cosmic geometry, matter density, and the
broad development of large-scale structure.
Independent CMB experiments recover compatible features over overlapping
observational ranges, strengthening the conclusion that the measured structures are not
artefacts of one instrument.
The correct scientific statement is therefore not that ΛCDM has been directly observed.
It is that ΛCDM is an extraordinarily successful model of a large body of observations.

0.42
A successful model is evidence about nature.
It is not identical to nature.
What the Evidence Establishes
The observational foundation developed in Chapter 1 remains intact.
The observable Universe is structured and evolving. Cosmological redshifts, distance
relations, baryon acoustic structure, the cosmic microwave background, primordial-
abundance information, gravitational lensing, gravitational waves, and other independent
observational channels jointly support a Universe that was once much hotter and denser
and whose large-scale geometry evolves with time.
Late-time acceleration is strongly supported within the relevant cosmological framework.
The combined gravitational evidence requires either a substantial additional non-luminous
gravitating component within the standard gravitational description or an alternative
gravitational theory capable of reproducing the same range of phenomena.
The Hubble tension is also real in the specific sense established earlier in this book:
materially different inference chains for the same parameter remain in conflict. Chapters 2
and 3 give the full numerical benchmarks, uncertainties, dependencies, and comparisons.
The local distance network, the combined CMB inference, and the DESI DR2 plus Big Bang
nucleosynthesis standard-ruler route remain distinct inference chains rather than three
identical instrument readings..

Several of the most important physical questions remain open.
The microscopic identity of dark matter is unknown.
The physical origin of late-time acceleration is unknown.
The Hubble tension remains unresolved.
No tested early-Universe mechanism in this investigation has been established as a

The full size and topology of the Universe are not determined by observations within our
causal region.
The physics of epochs earlier than those directly constrained by the cosmic microwave
background and nucleosynthesis becomes progressively more model dependent.
The Planck likelihood investigation has also left a methodological problem unresolved.
CamSpec and HiLLiPoP produce materially different fitted parameter geometry on

What the Computational Programme Did Not Establish
The computational programme did not establish a mathematical proof of the global
minimum of every tested high-H₀ profile.
It did not establish that Planck, SPT-3G, or ACT is incorrect.
It did not establish a faulty Planck calibration.
It did not identify a defective frequency channel.
It did not demonstrate an unmodelled foreground systematic.
It did not establish a covariance defect.
It did not demonstrate that optimizer basins correspond to physically distinct cosmologies.
It did not establish that the historical and later stable-basin labels are directly
interchangeable, because the historical continuation and the later portability and
revalidation programme used different operational classifier definitions. Nor did the
matched primordial-freeze isolation establish that primordial parameterization is globally
irrelevant to the historical-versus-later discrepancy; it established only that freezing the
primordial block does not recover repeated stable basins under the later common-
geometry classifier.
It did not turn repeated internal analyses of the same observational likelihoods into
independent observational evidence.
It did not establish whether the CamSpec–HiLLiPoP fitted-geometry difference produces a
material downstream cosmological difference, collapses to effectively equivalent
constraints, or is conditional on the external dataset combination.
The completed V19 matrix also did not execute the broader planned ΛCDM-versus-EDE
comparison with a common supernova block, so its controlled no-science outcome does not
close that broader scientific question.
It did not demonstrate new physics.

Q042 is now closed with a documented inconclusive feasibility answer. The strategies actually tested did not establish execution
and scientific qualification of that original contract. This is not an affirmative production result and it is not an impossibility
theorem: feasibility of a materially different contract-preserving strategy remains unresolved.
The final V31 branch produced partial conditional histories rather than a complete physical reference. Both bounded attempts
stopped at the parent 600-second deadline, and no complete tau value was computed. Under the frozen source extension, global
rails and append-only retention of the existing certified intervals, however, a separate necessary output-policy obstruction was

Glossary
AIC — Akaike information criterion. A model-comparison statistic that balances goodness
of fit against the number of fitted parameters. A lower AIC can favor one tested model over
another, but it is not proof that the model is physically correct.
APlanck — A Planck calibration parameter used in several likelihood analyses in this book.
It is a fitted nuisance or calibration quantity, not a separate cosmological observable.
Accepted model — The Bubbleverse-model state that has passed the repository’s required
promotion and validation process. Candidate content is not accepted merely because it
exists.
ACT — Atacama Cosmology Telescope. A ground-based experiment that measures cosmic
microwave background temperature and polarization structure at high angular resolution.
Angular power spectrum — A statistical description of how fluctuations on a sky map are
distributed across angular scales. CMB temperature and polarization measurements are
commonly expressed through TT, TE and EE power spectra.
BAO — Baryon acoustic oscillations. A preferred clustering scale measured statistically in
the distribution of matter. Using it as an absolute standard ruler requires calibration of its
physical scale.
BBN — Big Bang nucleosynthesis. The early-Universe nuclear process used to predict
primordial light-element abundances within a specified physical model.
Best-observed minimum — The lowest likelihood or objective value actually found in the
available numerical search. It is not automatically a proven global minimum.
Bubbleverse — The observation-first research framework used in this book to separate
measured signals, reconstruction, inference and physical interpretation while preserving a
traceable question-by-question research record.
Bubbleverse-model — The dedicated version-controlled repository that stores accepted
and candidate Bubbleverse model states, formal model files, tests, provenance and version
history.
Calibration — The process of relating an instrument or astrophysical indicator to a known
physical scale. Calibration uncertainty can propagate into inferred cosmological
parameters.
CamSpec — A Planck CMB likelihood implementation used in the Planck analyses
examined in this book.
Candidate model — A proposed next Bubbleverse-model state undergoing validation. A
candidate may pass, fail, remain blocked, be revised or be superseded.
CASE — A Bubbleverse provenance identifier used to group a scientific problem or
investigation. CASE identifiers are primarily bookkeeping and traceability devices.

CMB — Cosmic microwave background. The nearly isotropic microwave radiation field
observed across the sky, including small temperature and polarization fluctuations.
Common backend — A deliberately shared computational framework used to compare
different models under a controlled set of likelihoods, datasets, priors and numerical
conventions. It should not be confused with an exact reproduction of every external
paper’s native pipeline.
Confidence interval — A statistical interval constructed according to a specified
procedure to express uncertainty in an estimated quantity. Its interpretation depends on
the method used to construct it.
Covariance — A description of how uncertainties or residuals vary together. Correlated
data cannot generally be treated as a collection of independent measurements.
Dark energy — The name given to the component, field, modification or effective
phenomenon used to reproduce accelerated late-time expansion in a cosmological model.
The name does not imply that a microscopic dark-energy substance has been directly
detected.
Dark matter — The standard description of the additional non-luminous gravitating
component required across a wide range of astrophysical and cosmological evidence under
conventional gravity. Its microscopic identity remains unknown.
DESI — Dark Energy Spectroscopic Instrument. A spectroscopic survey used in this book
for large-scale-structure and baryon-acoustic-oscillation constraints.
Distance ladder — A chain of calibrated distance measurements in which nearby
geometric anchors calibrate more distant indicators, eventually allowing the late-Universe
expansion rate to be inferred.
EDE — Early dark energy. A class of models in which an additional energy component
contributes during an early cosmic epoch and may alter the sound horizon and inferred
expansion parameters.
EE — The CMB E-mode polarization auto-spectrum.
Evidence chain — The sequence linking an observed signal through calibration, modelling
and statistical inference to a scientific conclusion.
fEDE — The fractional early-dark-energy contribution used in the EDE analyses in this
book.
Foreground — Astrophysical emission or contamination that must be modelled or
separated from the cosmological signal of interest.
Global minimum — The lowest possible value of an objective function over the complete
permitted parameter space. Numerical optimization may find a low minimum without
proving that it is the global minimum.
GitHub Actions — GitHub’s automated workflow system. Bubbleverse uses Actions to
execute and preserve many numerical programmes and validation pipelines.

H0 — The Hubble constant, the present value of the cosmic expansion parameter H(t). It is
inferred from observations through a physical and statistical chain rather than read
directly from an instrument.
H(z) — The expansion parameter evaluated at redshift z. Different observational methods
constrain it with different calibration and modelling dependencies.
HiLLiPoP — A Planck PR4/NPIPE likelihood implementation independently compared with
CamSpec in the later Bubbleverse computational programme.
Hubble tension — The statistically significant disagreement among leading inference
chains for the present expansion parameter H0. The existence of the discrepancy does not
by itself establish its cause or prove new physics.
Inference — A conclusion derived from observations through calibration, modelling,
statistics or physical assumptions. Bubbleverse deliberately distinguishes inference from
the raw signal recorded by an instrument.
Inference chain — The complete route from observed quantities through calibrations and
modelling assumptions to an inferred parameter or conclusion.
JAGB — J-region asymptotic giant branch stars. A stellar distance indicator investigated as
an alternative calibration route within the late-Universe distance network.
ΛCDM — Lambda cold dark matter. The standard compact cosmological model containing
a cosmological constant, cold dark matter and the usual early-Universe framework. Its
empirical success does not make every component a directly observed microscopic entity.
Likelihood — A statistical function describing how compatible specified model parameters
are with the observed data under a defined statistical model.
Likelihood architecture — The complete construction of a likelihood, including its data
vector, covariance, retained information, nuisance parameters, calibration structure,
foreground model and statistical implementation.
Marginalization — A statistical procedure that removes nuisance parameters from the
quantity of interest by integrating or otherwise accounting for their allowed values.
Megamaser — Intense microwave emission from molecular gas. Disk megamasers can
provide geometric galaxy distances and therefore an alternative route to H0.
Mikami — Bubbleverse’s scientific triage and revision stage used before the Thin Red Line
editing pass. In this book it is a provenance term describing where claims were retained,
constrained, rejected or sent to the graveyard.
Model dependence — Dependence of an inferred result on the physical or statistical
framework used to translate observations into quantities that are not measured directly.
Multipole, ℓ — The conventional index used to describe angular scale in a spherical-
harmonic analysis. Larger ℓ generally corresponds to smaller angular scales.
NPIPE — A Planck data-processing product used in several later Planck likelihood analyses
discussed in this book.

Nuisance parameter — A fitted parameter required to describe calibration, foregrounds
or other non-target effects but which is not itself the main scientific quantity being
inferred.
Observation — A signal or quantity recorded from the physical world. Bubbleverse uses
the term more narrowly than inference or physical interpretation.
Optimizer basin — A region of parameter space toward which a numerical optimizer can
converge. Different starting points may reach different basins.
Pantheon+ — A large Type Ia supernova compilation used in late-Universe distance and
cosmological analyses discussed in this book.
Parameter degeneracy — A situation in which different combinations of model
parameters can produce similar observational predictions, making the parameters difficult
to constrain separately.
Peculiar velocity — Motion of an object relative to the idealized large-scale cosmological
expansion. Peculiar velocities matter particularly for low-redshift distance and H0
inference.
Planck — The ESA space mission that measured the CMB temperature and polarization sky
with high precision.
Precision matrix — The inverse of a covariance matrix. It appears in covariance-aware
likelihood and residual calculations.
Prior — Information or constraints placed on parameters before or alongside the
likelihood in a Bayesian analysis.
Profile likelihood — A likelihood construction in which parameters not currently being
examined are reoptimized at each tested value of the parameter of interest.
Provenance — The traceable record connecting a scientific claim or numerical result to its
source data, programme, configuration, workflow, commit, artifact or journal entry.
Q — A numbered Bubbleverse research question. Q numbers preserve chronological
research provenance; they are not the chapter structure of the main book.
Q firewall — The Bubbleverse-model rule that scientific model state may use only evidence
inside the currently authorized Q range.
Q-Journal — The chronological record of a Bubbleverse research question, including its
starting state, hypotheses, methods, results, failures, model changes, provenance and
handoff.
Redshift, z — The fractional shift of a spectral wavelength between emission and
observation. Cosmological redshift is directly observed through spectra; its conversion into
distances, ages or an expansion history requires additional modelling.
Reproducibility — The degree to which a calculation can be reconstructed from its data,
code, environment, configuration, workflow and recorded provenance.

Result ID — A stable Bubbleverse identifier attached to a specific computational or
scientific result.
R_d — The sound horizon associated with the baryon-drag epoch. It acts as the physical
ruler used in standard interpretations of BAO.
RQMC — Randomized quasi-Monte Carlo. A numerical integration method used in the
common CMB-space nuisance-marginalization test without relying on the single-Gaussian
approximation. The tested finite implementation did not pass the required numerical-
stability criteria.
Scale factor, a(t) — The quantity used in homogeneous and isotropic cosmology to
describe the relative cosmic scale as a function of time.
SH0ES — The distance-ladder programme using geometric anchors, Cepheid variables,
Type Ia supernovae and Hubble-flow objects to infer H0.
Sound horizon — The characteristic distance travelled by acoustic disturbances in the
early photon–baryon plasma before the relevant decoupling epoch. Its calibrated size is
central to BAO and early-Universe inference.
SPT-3G — The third-generation South Pole Telescope camera and its CMB measurements.
Standard candle — An object whose luminosity can be standardized or otherwise
calibrated so that its observed brightness provides distance information.
Standard ruler — A physical scale whose calibrated size allows observed angular or
redshift separations to provide cosmological distance information.
Standard siren — A gravitational-wave source whose waveform provides luminosity-
distance information. Combined with redshift information, it can constrain H0.
Superseded result — A historical result retained for provenance after a later analysis
provides a corrected or more authoritative result. Superseded does not mean that the
historical calculation never occurred.
TE — The cross-spectrum between CMB temperature fluctuations and E-mode polarization.
Thin Red Line — The Bubbleverse editorial boundary that permits restructuring,
compression and clarification while forbidding unauthorized changes to the surviving
scientific state.
TRGB — Tip of the red-giant branch. A stellar luminosity feature used as a distance
indicator and alternative calibration route.
TT — The CMB temperature auto-spectrum.
Uncertainty — A quantitative description of the range or distribution of values consistent
with an analysis. Statistical, systematic and other uncertainty components must not be
silently merged when the source keeps them separate.
World Breaker / Hulk Smash — Bubbleverse falsification and contradiction-testing stages
used before the final structural editing pass. They belong to the research workflow rather
than to the cosmological model itself.
